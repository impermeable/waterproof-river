In this section, we will one by one introduce the concepts that occur in the completeness axiom. 
All these concepts make sense for any totally ordered field. Throughout this section, we will denote by $\mathbf{R}$ an arbitrary totally ordered field. We use the slight typographic difference between `$\mathbb{R}$' and `$\mathbf{R}$' to allow you to think of $\mathbf{R}$ as the real line, but in principle it could be another totally ordered field as well, such as the rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$. However, as soon as you assume that a totally ordered field $\mathbf{R}$ satisfies the completeness axiom, then it is just the same as the real numbers $\mathbb{R}$.

We first define what we mean by upper and lower bounds of subsets of a totally ordered field.

\begin{definition}[Upper bound and lower bound]
	We say a number $M \in \mathbf{R}$ is an \emph{upper bound} for a subset $A \subset \mathbf{R}$ if
	\[
	\begin{split}
		&\foralltext a \in A,\\
		&\qquad	a \leq M.
	\end{split}
	\]
	
	We say a number $m \in \mathbf{R}$ is a \emph{lower bound} for a subset $A \subset \mathbf{R}$ if 
	\[
	\begin{split}
		&\foralltext a \in A\\
		&\qquad m \leq a.
	\end{split}
	\]
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
Let $A := (0, 2) \cup (3, 6)$ be a subset of $\mathbb{R}$. Then $8$ is an upper bound for $A$. Let us prove this according to best practices.

We need to show that for all $a \in A$, $a \leq 8$.

Let $a \in A$. We need to show that $a \leq 8$. Since $a \in (0, 2) \cup (3, 6)$, it indeed holds that $a \leq 8$.

\end{example}

Given the definition of upper and lower bounds, we define what it means for a set to be bounded from above, bounded from below and just bounded.

\begin{definition}[bounded from above, bounded from below, bounded]
	We say that a subset $A \subset \mathbf{R}$ is \emph{bounded from above} if there exists an $M \in \mathbf{R}$ such that $M$ is an upper bound for $A$.
	
	We say that a subset $A \subset \mathbf{R}$ is \emph{bounded from below} if there exists an $m \in \mathbf{R}$ such that $m$ is a lower bound for $A$.
	
	We say that a subset $A \subset \mathbf{R}$ is \emph{bounded} if $A$ is both bounded from above and bounded from below.	
\end{definition}

A \emph{least upper bound} is an upper bound that is smaller than or equal to any other upper bound.

\begin{definition}[least upper bound a.k.a.~supremum]
	Precisely, $M$ is a \emph{least upper bound} of a subset $A$ if both
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \label{it:lub-cond-1} $M$ is an upper bound
	\item \label{it:lub-cond-2} For every upper bound $L \in \mathbf{R}$ of $A$, it holds that $M \leq L$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
Suppose both $M$ and $W$ are a least upper bound of a subset $A \subset \mathbf{R}$.
Then $M = W$. In other words, the least upper bound is unique.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
	Since $M$ is a least upper bound, and $W$ is an upper bound, we know by item (\ref{it:lub-cond-2}) in the characterization of the least upper bound that $M \leq W$. Similarly, $W$ is a least upper bound, and $M$ is an upper bound, so $W \leq M$. It follows that $M = W$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[The supremum]
A different name for the least upper bound of a set $A \subset \mathbf{R}$ is the \emph{supremum} of $A$, which we also write as $\sup A$. 
\end{definition}

Given all the terminology defined above, we can now state the completeness axiom.

\begin{axiom}[Completeness axiom]
	\label{ax:completeness}
	We say that a totally ordered field $\mathbf{R}$ satisfies the \emph{completeness axiom} if every non-empty subset of $\mathbf{R}$ that is bounded from above has a \emph{least upper bound}.	
\end{axiom}

As soon as the completeness axiom is satisfied, we can also derive that every nonempty subset that is bounded from below has a largest lower bound.

\begin{lemma}
	\label{le:exists-llb}
	Every non-empty subset of the real line that is bounded from below has a \emph{largest lower bound}.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
	Let $A$ be a non-empty subset of the real line that is bounded from below.
	We need to show that there exists an $m \in \mathbf{R}$ such that
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \label{it:exists-inf-1} $m$ is a lower bound of $A$.
		\item \label{it:exists-inf-2} for every lower bound $l$ of $A$, it holds that $l \leq m$.
	\end{enumerate}
	We define $B := - A$, where by $-A$ we mean
	\[
	-A := \{-a \ | \ a \in A \}.
	\]
	Then $B$ is nonempty, and $B$ is bounded from above.
	Therefore, $B$ has a smallest upper bound $M = \sup B$.
	We choose $m := - M$. We will show (\ref{it:exists-inf-1}) and (\ref{it:exists-inf-2}).
	
	We first show (\ref{it:exists-inf-1}), in other words we need to show that
	\[
	\begin{split}
	& \foralltext a \in A \\
	& \quad m \leq a.	
	\end{split}
	\]
	Let $a \in A$. Because $M$ is the supremum of $B$, we know that for all $b \in B$, it holds that $b \leq M$. 
	In particular, since $-a \in -A = B$, we know that $-a \leq M = -m$. Therefore indeed $m \leq a$.
	
	We now show (\ref{it:exists-inf-2}).
	Let $l$ be a lower bound for $A$.
	Then $-l$ is an upper bound for $-A = B$. Because $M$ is the supremum of $B$, we know that $M \leq -l$. Recall that $M = -m$. Therefore $-m \leq -l$ and $l \leq m$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
	We usually call the largest lower bound of a non-empty set $A \subset \mathbf{R}$ that is bounded from below the \emph{infimum} of $A$, and we denote it by $\inf A$.
\end{definition}

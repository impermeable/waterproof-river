In this section we are going to define a function (called the lim sup) that takes in a real-valued sequence and outputs either
\begin{itemize}
\item the symbol ``$\infty$" if the sequence is not bounded from above
\item the symbol ``$-\infty$", if the sequence diverges to $-\infty$
\item a real number otherwise.
\end{itemize}


Consider a real-valued sequence $(a_n)$ that is bounded from above and does not diverge to $-\infty$.
We can then define a new sequence
\[
k \mapsto \sup_{n \geq k} a_n.
\]
Note that this sequence is decreasing, because for larger $k$ the supremum is taken over a smaller set. We will show in the lemma below that then the sequence $k \mapsto \sup_{n \geq k} a_n$ is also bounded from below. 

Therefore, the sequence $k \mapsto \sup_{n \geq k} a_n$ has a limit, and the limit is in fact equal to the infimum of the sequence. This limit is called the $\limsup$
\[
\begin{split}
	\limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n &:= 
	\inf_{k \in \mathbb{N}} \sup_{n \geq k} a_n\\
	&= \lim_{k \to \infty} \left( \sup_{n \geq k} a_n \right).
\end{split}
\]

We still need to show the announced lemma.

\begin{lemma}
	Let $a : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$ be a sequence that is bounded from above and does not diverge to $-\infty$.
	Then the sequence $k \mapsto \sup_{n \geq k} a_n$ is bounded from below.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We argue by contradiction. Suppose therefore that the sequence $k \mapsto \sup_{n \geq k} a_n$ is not bounded from below. We are going to show that the sequence $a_n$ diverges to $-\infty$, which would indeed be a contradiction.

We will show that 
\[
\begin{split}
&\foralltext M \in \mathbb{R} \\
&\quad \existstext N \in \mathbb{N} \\
&\quad\quad \foralltext n \geq N,\\
&\quad\quad\quad a_n < M.
\end{split}
\]
Let $M \in \mathbb{R}$.
Since the sequence $k \mapsto \sup_{n \geq k} a_n$ is not bounded from below, there exists an $m \in \mathbb{N}$, such that
\[
\sup_{n \geq m} a_n < M
\]
Choose $N := m$. Let $n \geq N$. Then
\[
a_n \leq \sup_{\ell \geq m} a_\ell < M.
\]
This finishes our proof that $(a_n)$ diverges to $-\infty$, and we have derived a contradiction. Hence the sequence $k \mapsto \sup_{n \geq k} (a_n)$ is in fact bounded from below.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Alternative characterization of $\limsup$]
Let $(a_n)$ be a real-valued sequence. Let $M \in \mathbb{R}$.
Then $M \in \mathbb{R}$ equals $\limsup_{\ell \to \infty} a_\ell$ if and only if the following two conditions hold:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \label{it:charlimsup1} 
\[
\begin{split}
&\foralltext \epsilon > 0,\\
&\quad \existstext N \in \mathbb{N},\\
&\quad\quad \foralltext \ell \geq N,\\
&\quad\quad\quad a_\ell < M + \epsilon.
\end{split}
\]
\item \label{it:charlimsup2}
\[
\begin{split}
&\foralltext \epsilon > 0,\\
&\quad \foralltext k \in \mathbb{N},\\
&\quad\quad \existstext m \geq k,\\
&\quad\quad\quad a_m > M - \epsilon.
\end{split}
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $M \in \mathbb{R}$.
We first show that if $M = \limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n$, then the conditions (\ref{it:charlimsup1}) and (\ref{it:charlimsup2}) hold.
Assume $M = \limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n$. Then by definition of the lim sup, it follows that $a: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$ is bounded from above and does not diverge to $-\infty$. 

We will now show (\ref{it:charlimsup1}).
Let $\epsilon > 0$.
We need to show that there exists a $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that for all $\ell \geq N$, it holds that $a_\ell < M + \epsilon$.

By the definition of $\limsup_{\ell \to \infty} a_\ell$ as $\inf_{\ell \in \mathbb{N}} \sup_{k \geq \ell} a_k$, there exists an $\ell_0 \in \mathbb{N}$ such that 
\[
\sup_{k \geq \ell_0} a_k < M + \epsilon.
\]
Choose $N := \ell_0$. Let $\ell \geq N$. Then by the previous inequality we conclude that
\[
a_\ell < M + \epsilon.
\]

We will now show (\ref{it:charlimsup2}).
Let $\epsilon > 0$. Let $k \in \mathbb{N}$. We need to show that there exists an $m \geq k$ such that $a_m > M - \epsilon$. By the definition of $\limsup_{\ell \to \infty} a_\ell$ as $\inf_{\ell \in \mathbb{N}} \sup_{k \geq \ell} a_k$, we know that 
\[
\sup_{n \geq k} a_n \geq M.
\]
Therefore, there exists an $m \geq k$ such that 
\[
a_m > \sup_{n \geq k} a_n - \epsilon \geq M - \epsilon.
\]

We will now show that if $M \in \mathbb{R}$ satisfies 
conditions (\ref{it:charlimsup1}) and (\ref{it:charlimsup2}), that 
then $M$ equals $\limsup_{\ell \to \infty} a_\ell$.

Assume $M$ satisfies conditions (\ref{it:charlimsup1}) and (\ref{it:charlimsup2}).

We first need to settle that $a: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$ is bounded from above and does not diverge to $-\infty$.

We will show that $a: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$ is bounded from above. By (\ref{it:charlimsup1}), we may obtain an $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that for all $\ell \geq N$, $a_\ell < M + 1$. Choose $L := \max(a_0, a_1, \dots, a_{N-1}, M+1)$. Then for all $\ell \in \mathbb{N}$, it holds that $a_\ell \leq L$. Hence $L$ is an upper bound for $a: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$ and $a: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$ is indeed bounded.

We will now show that $a: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$ does not diverge to $-\infty$. We argue by contradiction. Suppose $a: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$ diverges to $-\infty$. Then there exists an $N_0 \in \mathbb{N}$ such that for all $\ell \geq N_0$, $a_\ell < M - 1$. However, by (\ref{it:charlimsup2}) there exists an $m \geq N_0$ such that $a_m > M - 1$. This is a contradiction.

It now follows that $\limsup_{\ell \to \infty} a_\ell = \inf_{k \in \mathbb{N}} \sup_{n \geq k} a_n$. We therefore need to show that
\[
M = \inf_{k \in \mathbb{N}} \sup_{n \geq k} a_n.
\]
We first show that for every $k \in \mathbb{N}$,
\[
M \leq \sup_{n \geq k} a_n.
\]
Let $k \in \mathbb{N}$. 
Note that it suffices to show that for every $\epsilon > 0$ 
\[
M - \epsilon < \sup_{n \geq k} a_n
\]
Let $\epsilon > 0$. 
By (\ref{it:charlimsup2}) we know that there exists an $m \geq k$ such that
\[
M - \epsilon < a_m.
\]
Therefore also 
\[
M - \epsilon < \sup_{n \geq k} a_n
\]

Finally, we show that for every $\epsilon > 0$,
there exists a $k \in \mathbb{N}$ such that
\[
\sup_{n \geq k} a_n < M + \epsilon
\]
Let $\epsilon > 0$.
By condition (\ref{it:charlimsup1}) we know that there exists an $n_0 \in \mathbb{N}$ such that for all $n \geq n_0$,
\[
a_n < M + \epsilon/2
\]
Choose $k := n_0$.
Then 
\[
\sup_{n \geq k} a_n \leq M + \epsilon/2 < M + \epsilon.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
	\label{th:subseq-to-lim-sup}
Let $a: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued sequence that is bounded from above and does not diverge to $-\infty$. Then $\limsup_{\ell \to \infty} a_\ell$ is a (sequential) accumulation point of the sequence $a$, i.e.~there exists a subsequence of $a$ that converges to $\limsup_{\ell \to \infty} a_\ell$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
We denote $\limsup_{k \to \infty} a_k$ by $M$.
	
We need to find an index sequence $n: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$ such that
\[
\lim_{k \to \infty} a_{n_k} = M.
\]
We do this inductively. We first know that there exists an $m_0$ such that for all $m \geq m_0$,
\[
a_m \leq M + 1/1.
\]
Then we know there exists an $n_0$ such that $n_0 > m_0$ and
\[
M - 1/1 < a_{n_0}
\]
Because $n_0 > m_0$, we also know that
\[
a_{n_0} < M + 1/1
\]

Suppose now that $n_{\ell-1}$ is defined for some $\ell \in \mathbb{N}\setminus \{0\}$.
We are going to define $n_{\ell}$.
We know that there exists an $m_\ell \in \mathbb{N}$ such that for every $m \geq m_\ell$,
\[
a_m \leq M + \frac{1}{\ell + 1}.
\]
Now, there exists an $n_{\ell} \geq \max(n_{\ell-1}, m_\ell) + 1$ such that 
\[
M - \frac{1}{\ell + 1} < a_{n_\ell}
\]
and because $n_\ell > m_\ell$, we also know that
\[
a_{n_\ell} < M + \frac{1}{\ell + 1}.
\]

By construction, we know that $n: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$ is strictly increasing. In other words, we know that it is an index sequence.
Also by construction, we know that for all $\ell \in \mathbb{N}$,
\[
M - \frac{1}{\ell+1} < a_{n_\ell} < M + \frac{1}{\ell + 1}.
\]
By the squeeze theorem, we know that the $\lim_{\ell \to \infty} a_{n_\ell}$ exists and equals $M$.
\end{proof}

The previous theorem has the following consequence, which is a key fact in analysis.

\begin{corollary}[Bolzano-Weierstrass]
	Every bounded, real-valued sequence has a subsequence that converges in $(\mathbb{R}, \dist_{\mathbb{R}})$.
\end{corollary}

Theorem \ref{th:subseq-to-lim-sup} shows that if a sequence $a: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$ is bounded from above and does not diverge to infinity, the number $\limsup_{\ell \to \infty} a_\ell$ is a sequential accumulation point. However, we can derive more: in fact it is the maximum of the set of accumulation points.

\begin{theorem}
Suppose a sequence $a : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$ is bounded from above and does not diverge to $-\infty$. Then 
\[
\limsup_{\ell \to \infty} a_\ell
\]	
is the maximum of the set of sequential accumulation points.
\end{theorem}
